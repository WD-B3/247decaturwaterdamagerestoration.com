How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Decatur, AL

A scope of work is the written list of what a contractor will do. Learn the terms before you sign.

Emergency Dispatch
(256) 667-9078
Call Now

By the Decatur Water Damage Pros team · Published November 10, 2026

A scope of work is the document that says what will be done, in which rooms and with what materials. It is the most important paper in a water damage job, because it is what you and your insurer will compare against the invoice.

Many scopes use short terms that are easy to skim over. This guide explains the common ones and the questions to ask.

Terms you will see

TermPlain meaning
ExtractionPulling standing and absorbed water out
Demo or tear-outRemoving materials that cannot be saved
Flood cutCutting drywall up a set height to dry the wall cavity
Air moverA fan that moves air across wet surfaces
LGR dehumidifierA low-grain refrigerant unit that pulls moisture from the air
ContainmentPlastic barriers to keep dust and moisture in an area
AntimicrobialA treatment applied to surfaces after cleaning
MonitoringTaking moisture readings on visits

Read the rooms and measurements

A scope should name each affected room and give measurements, such as square feet of flooring or linear feet of drywall. Vague entries like "bathroom damage" make it hard to check later.

Walk through the home with the estimator. Point to areas that you think are wet and ask whether they are on the list. If a room is missing, ask why.

Look at what is removed and what is saved

Removal is the line to read most closely. A scope that removes everything may be more than needed. One that removes too little may leave wet material behind. Ask for the reason for each removal, and ask what moisture readings supported it.

Salvage decisions depend on the water category. Clean-water carpet pad is often replaced and carpet may be saved. Contaminated water changes that. See carpet water damage for an example.

Drying is its own section

A good scope has a drying section with the number of units, the days and how often they are checked. It should state the target, such as readings returning to the normal range for the material.

If drying is lumped into a general fee, ask for the detail. You cannot check what is not written.

Rebuild and finish lines

Rebuild lines cover drywall, insulation, flooring, paint and trim. Look for specifics, such as the finish, the paint coats and whether baseboards are reused. "Paint as needed" is too vague.

If rebuild is separate, the scope should say so and say who does it. Some companies do mitigation only and refer the rebuild.

Exclusions and allowances

Exclusions tell you what is not covered. Common ones include mold testing, plumbing repair, contents cleaning and permit fees. Allowances are set amounts for items whose price is not yet known, such as flooring.

An allowance can be fine, but you should know the amount and what happens if the real cost is higher.

Red flags

  • No room names or measurements.
  • No drying plan or equipment count.
  • A lump sum with no line items.
  • Pressure to sign the same day.
  • A request for full payment before work starts.
  • A direct payment assignment you did not request.

Ask about change orders

A scope can change once walls are opened. A change order is a written update that lists the new work and the reason. Ask how they are approved, who signs and how fast you will hear about one.

Never agree to a change by text alone. Ask for the update in writing with the room, the material and the reading that supports it.

Take photos and keep a copy

Photograph the affected rooms before work starts and keep a signed copy of the scope. Compare it with the invoice at the end. Every line on the invoice should trace back to a line in the scope or a written change order.

If a charge appears that you cannot find, ask about it before you pay.

Questions that reveal gaps

  • Which rooms are in the scope and which are not?
  • What happens if readings stay high after the planned days?
  • Is contents handling included?
  • Who disposes of debris?
  • What is the payment schedule?
  • How and when will I get the drying log?

Why plain language matters

If a scope uses terms you do not know, ask for a plain explanation. A company that wants you informed will gladly explain. One that avoids questions leaves you guessing about what you are paying for.

What Decatur homeowners should know

If your repair is large, the Alabama Home Builders Licensure Board defines a residential home builder by a $10,000 total cost threshold, and the board provides a public license lookup. A scope that crosses that line is worth checking against the lookup.

The City of Decatur Building Department can say whether your project needs a permit. Residents of Somerville and Tanner should ask their local office, since the authority differs.

Decatur questions answered

What is a scope of work?

A written list of what a contractor will do, in which rooms and with what materials. It is the main document you and your insurer compare to the invoice.

What does flood cut mean?

Cutting drywall up the wall at a set height to expose and dry the cavity behind it.

What is an allowance in a scope?

A set amount for an item whose price is not yet known, such as flooring. Ask what happens if the real cost is higher.

Should a scope include drying details?

Yes. It should list equipment, days and how drying will be verified.

Can I ask the contractor to explain each line?

Yes. A good contractor will walk through the scope with you and answer questions.
